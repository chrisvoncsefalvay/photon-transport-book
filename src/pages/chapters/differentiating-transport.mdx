Move a bone slightly across the beam. The primary image changes because rays cross different thicknesses of tissue. In the scattered image, moving the bone changes where photons can collide and which outgoing directions lead to the detector. A history that missed the detector before the move may now contribute. An absorbed photon might instead scatter. The derivative has to account for those changes in probability, although the simulation only records the history that actually happened.

A transport program can produce the right average image while its automatically differentiated version produces the wrong average gradient. Every multiplication may have been differentiated correctly. The missing contribution lives in the decisions between those multiplications: which interaction occurred, which boundary was crossed, which detector pixel received the photon.

We need a derivative of the expected measurement to decide how to change the object or the source. That requires choosing a representation of the expectation, deriving an estimator for its derivative, and arranging the GPU calculation around that estimator. The probability calculation comes first. Giving a photon history a tape does not make it confess to the paths it did not take.

Build the derivative by extending [Chapter 9's history walk](/chapters/scattering-stochastic-transport/#listing-9-2). For material-density changes in fixed cells, accumulate the likelihood contribution along each history. For a source-amplitude change, differentiate the multiplier on its score. These estimates will feed a stochastic inverse solver whose signal and derivative calculations use independent histories.

## 10.1 Differentiate an expected measurement

Take one detector pixel $p$ and one scalar parameter $\theta$. It might be a material coefficient, a translation in mm, an angle in radians or a source-spectrum parameter. Other parameters are held fixed during this partial derivative. Write $H$ for the physical history of one emitted photon, including its terminal event. Its normalised physical probability density is $f_\theta(H)$, and $S_p(H,\theta)$ is its detector score. The integral over histories includes sums over discrete choices and history lengths as well as integrals over continuous states.

Let $N_{\mathrm{src}}$ be the expected number of photons emitted in the exposure. For now, hold it fixed. The expected signal is

$$
y_p(\theta) =N_{\mathrm{src}}\,\mathbb E_{f_\theta}[S_p] =N_{\mathrm{src}}\int S_p f_\theta\,dH.
$$

<EquationNote term="y_p">expected signal in pixel p</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="N_{\mathrm{src}}">
  expected photons emitted in the exposure
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="S_p">detector score of one physical history</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="f_\theta">normalised physical history density</EquationNote>

In [equation (10.1)](#equation-10-1), the detector score carries the measurement units. A score of one for an accepted arrival gives expected counts, while an energy-dependent detector response gives that response's output units. The source and score conventions are those of [Chapter 9](/chapters/scattering-stochastic-transport/#94-sources-boundaries-and-detector-scores). The simulated history count $N$ controls the precision of an estimate of this integral. It does not replace $N_{\mathrm{src}}$ or change the exposure.

Both factors inside the integral can depend on the parameter. Differentiating a detector gain changes the score. Differentiating an interaction coefficient changes the history law. For a fixed history domain, suppose the integrand is differentiable in a neighbourhood of the parameter and its derivative has an integrable bound independent of the parameter in that neighbourhood. Differentiation can then pass under the integral. Where $f_\theta$ is positive, define its logarithmic derivative, or _likelihood score_, and apply the product rule:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathfrak s_\theta(H) =\partial_\theta\log f_\theta(H),\\
\partial_\theta y_p =N_{\mathrm{src}}\,\mathbb E_{f_\theta} \bigl[\partial_\theta S_p +S_p\mathfrak s_\theta\bigr].
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\mathfrak s_\theta">
  likelihood score with inverse parameter units
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\partial_\theta S_p">
  score derivative at fixed history coordinates
</EquationNote>

The two terms in [equation (10.2)](#equation-10-2) change the value assigned to a fixed history and the probability of drawing it. Here $\partial_\theta S_p$ holds the history coordinates fixed. The likelihood score has units reciprocal to those of $\theta$. It can be negative even when every photon contribution is non-negative. It is distinct from the detector score $S_p$.

Changing support or moving a jump in $S_p$ requires further treatment in §10.4. Those cases violate the simple smooth-integrand argument above. If the expected emitted population also varies, the product rule adds $(\partial_\theta N_{\mathrm{src}})\mathbb E[S_p]$. Keeping that prefactor explicit prevents a source-intensity gradient from disappearing into a normalised probability density.

## 10.2 Pathwise and score-function estimators

A random number generator supplies variables with a parameter-independent distribution. For example, a uniform variate remains uniform when the material density changes, while the sampled flight distance changes through the transformation applied to that variate. Suppose a history can be generated as $H=h_\theta(U)$ from base randomness $U$ with law $r$. If the transformed score is differentiable and its difference quotients admit an integrable bound, the _pathwise_ construction gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
A_p(\theta,U) =S_p(h_\theta(U),\theta),\\
\partial_\theta y_p =N_{\mathrm{src}}\,\mathbb E_r [\partial_\theta A_p].
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="A_p">
  detector score expressed through base randomness
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="h_\theta">
  map from base randomness to a history
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="U">parameter-independent base randomness</EquationNote>

In [equation (10.3)](#equation-10-3), the random numbers stay fixed while the sampled trajectory moves. The total derivative of $A_p$ includes the changing positions, energies and detector response along that trajectory. This is where automatic differentiation can carry a large amount of useful arithmetic.

Mohamed and colleagues organise gradient estimation around pathwise, likelihood-score and measure-valued constructions, with different regularity and support requirements. For our walker, a likelihood score accounts for the changing probability of collision events without differentiating a categorical branch. Smooth transformations can use pathwise derivatives, while moving discontinuities need the treatment in §10.4. The choice follows the dependence of the measurement on the parameter. <Citation keys={["mohamed2020"]} />

The pathwise and likelihood-score constructions represent the same derivative under their respective conditions. Adding their complete answers would count it twice. They can, however, handle different choices within one estimator. Consider a discrete choice $D$ with probabilities $q_\theta(d)$, followed by a continuous sample generated from independent base randomness. Let $A_p(\theta,d,U)$ be the resulting score. Differentiating the sum over $d$ and then its conditional integral gives

$$
\partial_\theta y_p =N_{\mathrm{src}}\,\mathbb E \bigl[\partial_\theta A_p +A_p\,\partial_\theta\log q_\theta(D)\bigr].
$$

<EquationNote term="A_p">score conditional on the discrete choice</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="q_\theta(D)">
  probability of the sampled discrete choice
</EquationNote>

The first term in [equation (10.4)](#equation-10-4) differentiates the continuous calculation with $D$ fixed. The second accounts for the changing probability of choosing $D$. A full history applies this reasoning conditionally at successive decisions. Each probability is conditioned on the preceding state, and its derivative includes the parameter dependence of that state under the chosen representation.

This division of labour is especially useful for transport. Flight lengths can sometimes be reparameterised smoothly. Absorption versus scattering is a categorical choice. A detector hit can introduce a jump even after every preceding sample was continuous. For each dependency, identify which estimator term covers it before building the backward calculation. The existence of a differentiable inverse sampler does not establish the smoothness of the final detector score.

## 10.3 Differentiate the sampling law

Start with a homogeneous medium whose total interaction coefficient is $\mu>0$, in mm$^{-1}$. A uniform variate $U\in(0,1)$ produces an unbounded free-flight distance $\ell$ in mm:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\ell=-\frac{\log U}{\mu},\\
\partial_\mu\ell=-\frac{\ell}{\mu}.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\ell">sampled free-flight distance, in mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mu">
  homogeneous interaction coefficient, in inverse mm
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="U">uniform variate between zero and one</EquationNote>

The negative derivative in [equation (10.5)](#equation-10-5) has a physical meaning: increasing the collision rate shortens the flight generated by the same random number. Its units are mm$^2$, because we differentiate a length with respect to an inverse length. The limit $\mu=0$ is the separate vacuum branch.

Now put the end of the medium at a fixed distance $d>0$. Record $C=1$ if a collision occurs before that end and $C=0$ if the photon escapes uncollided. The travelled distance is $\ell=\min(-\log U/\mu,d)$. The collision branch has density $\mu e^{-\mu\ell}$, while the escape branch has probability $e^{-\mu d}$. Their likelihood scores fit into one expression:

$$
\mathfrak s_\mu(C,\ell) =\frac{C}{\mu}-\ell.
$$

<EquationNote term="C">one for a collision, and zero for escape</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\ell">
  distance travelled before collision or escape, in mm
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathfrak s_\mu">
  likelihood derivative with respect to the interaction coefficient
</EquationNote>

[Equation (10.6)](#equation-10-6) includes the escape probability. Treating every recorded segment as though it ended in a collision would add a spurious $1/\mu$ on escaping histories. For a primary-count score $S_p=1-C$, only escapes contribute, so the expected per-emitted-photon derivative is

$$
\begin{gathered}
T=e^{-\mu d},\\
\mathbb E[S_p\mathfrak s_\mu] =-dT=\partial_\mu T.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="T">uncollided survival probability</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="d">fixed medium thickness, in mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="S_p">
  one for an uncollided escape, and zero otherwise
</EquationNote>

This recovers [Chapter 2's homogeneous transmission derivative](/chapters/transmission/#23-the-homogeneous-path) in [equation (10.7)](#equation-10-7) directly from a random-history estimator. At $\mu=0.2\ \mathrm{mm}^{-1}$ and $d=10\ \mathrm{mm}$, $T=e^{-2}$ and $\partial_\mu T=-10e^{-2}\ \mathrm{mm}$. Differentiating the realised binary escape score through the comparison instead would return zero almost surely. The expectation changes because the threshold selects a different fraction of random numbers.

In a heterogeneous medium, an uncollided segment carries the survival factor for its whole traversed length. Hold the segment geometry fixed for a material derivative. Write $\mu_t(s,\theta)$ for the coefficient at $\mathbf x+s\boldsymbol\omega$ along that segment, and define

$$
\begin{gathered}
\tau(\ell,\theta) =\int_0^\ell\mu_t(s,\theta)\,ds,\\
\partial_\theta\log e^{-\tau} =-\int_0^\ell\partial_\theta\mu_t(s,\theta)\,ds.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\tau">optical depth along the segment</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mu_t">
  total local interaction coefficient, in inverse mm
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\ell">fixed segment length, in mm</EquationNote>

[Equation (10.8)](#equation-10-8) contributes on every segment, including the last segment to the detector or domain boundary. A collision adds the logarithmic derivative of its local rate. For event type $a$, it is often convenient to factor this into a total collision rate and a conditional event probability:

$$
\begin{gathered}
q_a=\frac{\mu_a}{\mu_t},\\
\partial_\theta\log q_a =\partial_\theta\log\mu_a -\partial_\theta\log\mu_t.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="q_a">conditional probability of event type a</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mu_a">
  interaction coefficient for event type a
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mu_t">total interaction coefficient</EquationNote>

Combining the two rate terms in [equation (10.9)](#equation-10-9) leaves $\partial_\theta\log\mu_a$, as it should. A scattering event also contributes the derivative of its normalised angular and energy distribution. The source distribution contributes its own term. Probabilities or rates equal to zero require an alternative representation or a suitable one-sided limit. Dividing by zero is not a derivative estimator.

The supported material parameter is the logarithm of a positive density multiplier. That multiplier scales both absorption and scattering coefficients for one selected material, leaving their ratio and the conditional scattering law unchanged. Under analogue sampling, a collision in that material therefore contributes one to the log-density likelihood score, while each traversed segment contributes minus its total optical thickness. The accumulator in [Listing 9.2](/chapters/scattering-stochastic-transport/#listing-9-2) includes the final escape segment as well as collision-ending segments. This is the material-wise version of [equations (10.6)](#equation-10-6) and [(10.8)](#equation-10-8), with the logarithmic chart's chain factor already included.

The derivative kernel replays that walk and combines its density accumulator with the complete detector contribution. The same entry point also handles direct and logarithmic source-amplitude derivatives under a fixed source law.

For a density derivative, `base_weight` is set to zero during the walk because the forward score is unused. The derivative is then formed directly from source weight, amplitude, optional energy and `density_score`, using mantissa/exponent factorisation. The analogue branch calls `product4`, while continuous absorption weighting calls `attenuated_product4` with the accumulated absorption depth. An underflowed forward tally cannot erase a representable derivative, and an unused primal overflow cannot invalidate one. The probability calculation still uses the actual density. Zeroing that temporary score does not detach the physical law.

The direct amplitude branch instead evaluates the base score with amplitude one. This gives the derivative even at amplitude zero without dividing by zero. The log-amplitude branch requires positive amplitude and retains its chain factor in the score. These are derivatives of an intensity multiplier. Changing the source's angular distribution, spectrum or sampling support would introduce different terms and is outside this interface.

Under [continuous absorption weighting](/chapters/scattering-stochastic-transport/#integrating-absorption-along-the-path), the density derivative has two sources. The scattering-path likelihood contributes $N_{s,m}-\int_{H\cap m}\kappa_s\,\mathrm ds$, where $N_{s,m}$ counts scattering events in the active material. Differentiating the explicit absorption weight contributes $-\int_{H\cap m}\kappa_a\,\mathrm ds$. Thus a complete detector contribution $X$ has derivative estimator $D_m=X[N_{s,m}-\int_{H\cap m}(\kappa_a+\kappa_s)\,\mathrm ds]$. The familiar total optical depth reappears, but its absorption part now comes from differentiating a weight. Treating every subtraction as a likelihood term would obscure which probability law we are differentiating.

This argument holds for positive density multipliers, fixed coefficient zeros, geometry and source law, and a density-independent conditional scattering law. Interchanging the derivative and expectation still needs an integrable derivative. In the free-electron Compton model, density changes collision rates while the conditional energy-angle law stays fixed. Each segment uses the coefficients at its own energy. To check that calculation independently, differentiate the primary-plus-single-scatter integral and bound the derivative of the omitted scattering orders. A bound on their detector score alone is insufficient.

A sampling proposal can simplify these terms. At a reference parameter $\theta_0$, choose a fixed history proposal $g$ that covers the physical integrand and its derivative throughout a neighbourhood. On a fixed domain with the same interchange conditions, importance sampling gives

$$
\partial_\theta y_p =N_{\mathrm{src}} \times\mathbb E_g \left[\frac{\partial_\theta(S_p f_\theta)}{g}\right].
$$

<EquationNote term="g">fixed proposal density over histories</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="f_\theta">
  physical history density at the current parameter
</EquationNote>

In [equation (10.10)](#equation-10-10), $g$ is held fixed while differentiating. The physical density $f_\theta$ still changes inside the numerator. Detaching a proposal means holding that sampling law fixed in the derivative. Detaching the physical attenuation or scattering factor would delete the sensitivity we are trying to estimate.

For null-collision tracking, one option is to hold a valid majorant fixed locally. The candidate process is then unchanged, but acceptance and rejection probabilities still depend on the material. A likelihood treatment includes both outcomes, or a weighted formulation derives their replacement explicitly. The majorant must bound the physical rate over the parameter neighbourhood being used. Changing a majorant or a queue rule is an algorithmic change whose effects must cancel in the expected physical measurement.

## 10.4 Moving boundaries and discontinuities

A single detector edge exposes what ordinary trace differentiation misses. Let a crossing coordinate $X$ be uniform over a strip of width $w$ in mm. An aperture accepts $X<a$, with $0<a<w$ and fixed $N_{\mathrm{src}}$. Its expected count is

$$
\begin{gathered}
S(X,a)=\mathbf1\{X<a\},\\
y(a)=N_{\mathrm{src}}\frac{a}{w},\\
y'(a)=\frac{N_{\mathrm{src}}}{w}.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="X">uniform crossing coordinate, in mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="a">aperture boundary coordinate, in mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="w">full strip width, in mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="N_{\mathrm{src}}">
  expected emitted photon population
</EquationNote>

For every sampled $X\ne a$, the local derivative of the indicator in [equation (10.11)](#equation-10-11) is zero. Yet moving the edge by a small positive amount admits an interval of previously rejected crossings. Its width shrinks with the perturbation, while the change in each admitted score remains one. Dividing by the perturbation leaves a finite contribution. The derivative resides on the moving edge.

The same argument works when the two sides carry different smooth contributions. Let $q_+(x,\theta)$ and $q_-(x,\theta)$ be signal densities per mm to the left and right of a moving boundary $a(\theta)$ in a fixed strip. Integrating each side and differentiating its endpoint gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
y=\int_0^a q_+\,dx+\int_a^w q_-\,dx,\\
y'=\int_0^a\partial_\theta q_+\,dx +\int_a^w\partial_\theta q_-\,dx +\bigl[q_+(a)-q_-(a)\bigr]a'.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="q_+">signal density on the left, per mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="q_-">signal density on the right, per mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="a'">
  boundary displacement per unit parameter change
</EquationNote>

The final term in [equation (10.12)](#equation-10-12) is the jump in contribution multiplied by boundary motion. It vanishes when the contributions agree across the boundary. Otherwise, differentiating the smooth pieces leaves it out. In more dimensions, the corresponding term integrates over the boundary and uses its normal velocity.

Transport contains several such boundaries: an object silhouette, the edge of a detector pixel, a collimator opening and the interface at which one material becomes another. A geometric interface need not create a jump in every chosen integrand, so identify the actual contribution on both sides. Fixed random numbers do not remove a jump. Nor does assigning a zero derivative to a branch comparison account for it.

[Chapter 5](/chapters/differentiating-projection/#54-discretise-and-differentiate) encountered this problem in a sampled line integral through a sharp interface. In a transport history, the discontinuity can occur at any interaction or detector decision.

There are several ways forward. Integrate a simple acceptance probability analytically, as in the strip example. Sample the boundary contribution alongside the smooth contribution. Or transform the integration variables so that the problematic boundary becomes fixed and differentiate the transformed integrand and its Jacobian. Each method needs a derivation for the source, geometry and detector score being used. Replacing a sharp detector edge with a smooth filter defines a different measurement unless that filter belongs to the physical response model.

Li and colleagues derive an edge-sampling estimator for differentiable rendering that evaluates the jump across visibility boundaries in addition to the smooth contribution. Their construction makes the missing term explicit: ordinary area samples almost surely miss the lower-dimensional boundary integral. The same mathematical issue appears in photon transport, although the material and detector model determine the boundary contribution that must be sampled. <Citation keys={["li2018edges"]} />

We will keep material interfaces, detector faces and the source distribution fixed when differentiating density and amplitude. Adding moving geometry requires the corresponding interface contribution derived here; changing the source or conditional scattering law requires its probability derivative. These are additions to the estimator before they are additions to the parameter vector.

## 10.5 Replay, random numbers and memory

A long history can visit many materials before contributing to a detector pixel. Reverse differentiation needs the states at which its local factors were evaluated. Storing every position, energy, probability and weight for every history makes the memory cost grow with total path length. Recomputing the first event, then the first two, then the first three soon repeats a great deal of work: regenerating every prefix takes quadratic time in history length. A replay scheme should specify which states it reconstructs and how often.

Path replay backpropagation, developed by Vicini and colleagues, reconstructs local quantities for reverse differentiation using invertible local scattering Jacobians. Their formulation takes work linear in path length and constant path-local memory. We also regenerate histories to save storage, but accumulate a likelihood score during each forward replay for one selected parameter. This simpler organisation suits a small parameter set; their reverse-mode construction targets gradients with respect to many scene parameters. <Citation keys={["vicini2021prb"]} />

Reproducibility starts with the identity of a history. Assign random draws to a source-history identifier, event index and sampling purpose, with a further draw index for rejection samplers. Splitting requires persistent descendant identifiers as well. Then compaction or a different GPU scheduling order can move work between threads without changing which variates belong to it. A global seed alone does not provide this property if thread scheduling determines who consumes the next number.

During a backward replay at unchanged parameters, reproduce the same physical and sampling decisions. Retain enough information to reconstruct proposal densities, roulette probabilities, detector contributions and local states. A replay that encounters different random decisions is differentiating another realised computation. Within a parameter perturbation, however, the same variates are allowed to produce different decisions: that change is part of the finite difference.

To assemble `derivative_histories`, start from resident source states and a `HistoryBatch`, then run the walker with the selected parameter's likelihood accumulation. Reusing a batch at unchanged inputs reconstructs its histories, using [the counter implementation in Listing 9.6](/chapters/scattering-stochastic-transport/#listing-9-6). No preceding forward image is needed to evaluate this derivative estimator.

Each call writes pixel, signed binary64 derivative and terminal status per original history. Several parameters can reuse those buffers sequentially. Memory is therefore proportional to histories plus detector outputs, with no history-by-event tape or retained material-by-history array, while work grows by a full replay for each active parameter. This is an intentional tradeoff for a small declared parameter set. It should be remeasured before extending the inverse problem to hundreds of material parameters.

Keep source and model arrays immutable until replay finishes, and reject a derivative batch containing a failed or truncated history. This manual first-order calculation reconstructs the histories needed by §10.3's estimator without recording a Warp tape.

For a central difference, let $Y_+$ and $Y_-$ be two simulation estimates made with common random numbers at $\theta+h$ and $\theta-h$. Write their variances as $V_+$ and $V_-$ and their covariance as $C$. The central difference $D_h$ satisfies

$$
\begin{gathered}
D_h=\frac{Y_+-Y_-}{2h},\\
\operatorname{Var}(D_h) =\frac{V_++V_--2C}{4h^2}.
\end{gathered}
$$

</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="Y_-">

</EquationNote>

</EquationNote>

It is a property to check: different rejection counts or highly changed paths can weaken the coupling. Both marginal simulations must still have the intended law.

Use independent groups of coupled simulations to estimate uncertainty. A single fixed set of histories can be excellent for comparing nearby parameters, but it is only one realisation. ## 10.6 Variance of the gradient estimator

Let $Z_h$ be the complete scalar derivative contribution from source history $h$, including the exposure factor and any associated boundary samples. For $N>1$ independent, identically distributed source histories with finite second moment, the gradient estimate and its estimated variance are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widehat g=\frac1N\sum_{h=1}^N Z_h,\\
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat g) =\frac{\sum_{h=1}^N(Z_h-\widehat g)^2}{N(N-1)}.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="Z_h">
  complete derivative contribution of source history h
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\widehat g">
  estimated scalar measurement derivative
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="N">number of independent source histories</EquationNote>

[Equation (10.14)](#equation-10-14) groups correlated descendants with their source history. If boundary samples use a separate independent estimator, estimate its variance separately and add it. Shared randomness introduces a covariance term. Approximate normal intervals require enough effectively independent contributions for the mean's sampling distribution to be well behaved. Rare, heavily weighted histories can make a small empirical standard error misleading.

A likelihood estimator often multiplies a noisy contribution by another noisy, signed quantity. Under the fixed-support regularity conditions, normalisation implies $\mathbb E[\mathfrak s_\theta]=0$. Subtracting a constant baseline $b$ from the detector score therefore preserves the likelihood term's expectation:

$$
\mathbb E[(S_p-b)\mathfrak s_\theta] =\mathbb E[S_p\mathfrak s_\theta].
$$

<EquationNote term="b">
  baseline independent of the current sampled history
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathfrak s_\theta">
  likelihood score of that history
</EquationNote>

The baseline in [equation (10.15)](#equation-10-15) changes variance without changing the mean. Expanding the second moment as a quadratic in $b$ gives the scalar baseline that minimises the variance of this likelihood term, when the denominator is positive:

$$
b_*= \frac{\mathbb E[S_p\mathfrak s_\theta^2]} {\mathbb E[\mathfrak s_\theta^2]}.
$$

<EquationNote term="b_*">
  variance-minimising baseline for the scalar likelihood term
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="S_p">
  detector score before baseline subtraction
</EquationNote>

[Equation (10.16)](#equation-10-16) weights the detector score by the squared likelihood score. Simply using the mean detector signal need not minimise variance. Estimate a baseline from independent pilot histories, or use a conditional baseline independent of the current random choice given its preceding state. Fitting a baseline to the same contribution without the appropriate correction can alter the expected gradient. In a custom derivative rule, the baseline supplies a control variate. Its own parameter dependence is not an extra physical score term.

<MarginNote label="A shared baseline">
  Different parameters generally have different optimal likelihood baselines.
  Sharing one baseline trades some of that variance reduction for a smaller
  amount of state.
</MarginNote>

Other variance reductions can exploit the transport structure. Evaluate a primary contribution and its derivative deterministically, then estimate the remaining scattered contribution with matching normalisation. Replace a binary detector decision by its conditional expected response when that integral is available. Such conditioning also removes the variance contributed by that decision. It may eliminate a discontinuity that made direct pathwise differentiation fail.

Compare uncertainty in physically scaled parameter coordinates. If $\theta=\theta_0+a z$, where $z$ is dimensionless and $a$ is a chosen physical scale, then $\partial_z y=a\,\partial_\theta y$. Both the gradient and its standard error acquire the same factor. A rotation derivative per radian and a translation derivative per mm cannot be compared by their raw magnitudes to decide which parameter is well constrained.

## 10.7 Independent gradient validation

The homogeneous escape example tests the sampling-law derivative without complicated geometry. The moving aperture tests the missing boundary term. A smoothly varying detector response tests continuous pathwise arithmetic. These examples diagnose different failures, so an agreement on one is insufficient grounds to skip the others. [Table 10.2](#table-10-2) pairs the controlled problems with their expected derivatives and tested dependencies.

For a general parameter direction, compare the estimator with central differences of expected measurements over a range of perturbations. Estimate those differences with common random numbers and independent replications. On a sufficiently smooth expectation, central-difference truncation error is quadratic in the perturbation. Its sampling error also depends on the coupling and the discontinuities of individual histories.

The aperture makes that dependence explicit. For $0<a-h<a+h<w$, one history contributes to the central difference only when $X$ lies in the interval of width $2h$ swept by the edge. Its derivative contribution is then $N_{\mathrm{src}}/(2h)$. The contribution's mean is exactly $N_{\mathrm{src}}/w$, and the variance of an $N$-history average is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat g_h) =\frac{N_{\mathrm{src}}^2}{N} \times\left(\frac{1}{2wh}-\frac{1}{w^2}\right).
$$

<EquationNote term="\widehat g_h">
  coupled aperture-gradient estimate at step h
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="w">uniform strip width, in mm</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="h">boundary perturbation, in mm</EquationNote>

[Equation (10.17)](#equation-10-17) grows as $1/(Nh)$ when $h$ becomes small. Common random numbers help, but they do not make an arbitrarily small perturbation useful. With too few histories, none may land in the swept strip. The finite difference is then zero even though the expected derivative is positive. Increasing $N$ at a fixed step and varying the step at a fixed $N$ distinguish that sampling failure from a missing estimator term.

Report the parameter and its units, perturbation, history count, seed grouping, analytic target where available, and uncertainty of the discrepancy. If the derivative estimate and finite difference share histories, estimate their paired difference directly so that their covariance is included. Use an independently derived formula or estimator as the reference: a finite difference of a fixed, discontinuous sampled trace can share the very omission being tested.

For a vector parameter, also use [Chapter 5's directional checks](/chapters/differentiating-projection/#56-independent-directional-checks) and the pairing between Jacobian-vector and transpose-Jacobian products. These algebraic checks catch component ordering, frame and accumulation mistakes. They complement the expectation checks above, which catch missing probability and boundary contributions even when the implemented linear algebra is internally consistent.

Begin the implementation checks with slab log density, where analytic survival gives an independent loss derivative. Repeat the estimate with independent batches and compare the discrepancy with its sampling uncertainty. Use the aperture example separately to check any boundary estimator you add.

## 10.8 A differentiable transport implementation

The transport interface needs to state what its derivative returns. Given physical parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$, an exposure definition and a random-stream specification, the forward calculation estimates the expected detector image $\mathbf y$. The derivative calculation should accept a fixed detector weight vector $\mathbf w$ and estimate the corresponding parameter sensitivity:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf J=\frac{\partial\mathbf y} {\partial\boldsymbol\theta},\\
\mathbb E[\widehat{\mathbf b}] =\mathbf J^\mathsf T\mathbf w.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\mathbf J">
  Jacobian of the expected detector image
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\widehat{\mathbf b}">
  estimated parameter transpose-Jacobian product
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathbf w">fixed detector weight vector</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol\theta">physical parameter vector</EquationNote>

[Equation (10.18)](#equation-10-18) allows the implementation to accumulate a transpose-Jacobian product without materialising every pixel-by-parameter entry. The fixed weights combine detector scores before their history derivatives are accumulated. A random weight computed from the same noisy image requires the additional expectation analysis in §10.9.

Construct the contraction one parameter at a time: average its sparse history derivatives into a reusable detector buffer, apply the fixed pixel weights and reduce on the device. This stores one derivative image, which is useful for inspecting spatial sensitivity, and avoids retaining the entire pixel-by-parameter Jacobian. If memory traffic warrants fusing the weighted contraction into the history tally, preserve the same per-history estimator and original-history denominator.

The parameter set determines where contributions accumulate. A spatial material-field derivative can touch many volume entries; a pose derivative has six components summed over histories, with the interface terms from §10.4. Choose output buffers after deriving those contributions. For pose, retain [Chapter 3's frame and translation-before-rotation ordering](/chapters/frames-geometry-motion/#36-pose-parameters-and-local-updates).

Warp provides array gradients and custom replay and gradient functions for the continuous arithmetic in an estimator. Dynamic loops can overwrite intermediate values needed by the reverse calculation, so explicitly store or reconstruct them. The [Warp differentiation documentation](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.17.0/docs/user_guide/differentiability.rst) explains those mechanisms and their limitations. Combine them with the probability and boundary terms derived for the measurement; allocate retained state according to what that backward calculation needs.

Allocate volume data, cross-section tables, source state, queues, tallies and gradient buffers before the repeated solve. Record the dtype of each buffer and of reductions, because signed contributions can cancel strongly. Clear accumulated gradients between independent evaluations. Keep random-stream identities attached to work items during compaction. Queue overflow and a maximum interaction count require an explicit continuation or unbiased termination scheme: dropping the remaining histories changes both the expected image and its gradient.

When choosing between storage and replay, measure state traffic, launches and host synchronisation alongside elapsed time. Inspect event divergence and detector-tally contention. Vary live histories and active parameters: a small path-local state can still accompany large source, detector and gradient arrays.

A revised sampler needs derivative weights for its revised proposal. A faster reduction may change the accuracy of signed sums through its accumulation order. Caching a physics query must retain the parameter dependence of its result. The forward and derivative calculations must continue to describe the same physical expectation.

## 10.9 Inverse problems with stochastic gradients

An unbiased image estimate and an unbiased image derivative do not automatically give an unbiased inverse-problem gradient. Consider one pixel with expected signal $y(\theta)$, observed value $d$ and squared-error objective $\mathcal L=\tfrac12(y-d)^2$. Let $\widehat y$ and $\widehat g$ be unbiased estimates of $y$ and $y'=\partial_\theta y$. Multiplying the estimated residual by the estimated derivative gives

$$
\mathbb E[(\widehat y-d)\widehat g] =(y-d)y' +\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat y,\widehat g).
$$

<EquationNote term="\widehat y">unbiased signal estimate</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\widehat g">
  unbiased signal-derivative estimate
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="y'">derivative of the expected signal</EquationNote>

The covariance in [equation (10.19)](#equation-10-19) is a gradient bias when the target is squared error against the expected image. Reusing the same photon histories generally correlates the two estimates. More histories may reduce this term, but its sign and size follow from the joint estimator. They cannot be inferred from two separate unbiasedness claims.

For this quadratic objective, independent batches provide a direct correction. Use batch $A$ for the signal estimate and batch $B$ for its derivative:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widehat{\mathcal L'} =(\widehat y_A-d)\widehat g_B,\\
\mathbb E[\widehat{\mathcal L'}] =(y-d)y'.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\widehat y_A">signal estimate from batch A</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\widehat g_B">
  independent derivative estimate from batch B
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\widehat{\mathcal L'}">
  estimated squared-error objective derivative
</EquationNote>

Independence factorises the expectation in [equation (10.20)](#equation-10-20). Within each batch, variance reduction and replay can still share random numbers according to their own estimator design. The two batches must remain independent conditional on the current parameters and any fixed pilot information.

Form the weighted residual from image A and multiply it by derivative image B in `independent_squared_gradient`. Its binary64 pixel contributions are then summed on CUDA. Give A and B non-overlapping history streams. Random source samples must also be independent between them; a fixed deterministic source may be shared. The call checks declared history ranges and source namespaces, so construct the source samples consistently with those declarations.

The companion `independent_squared_loss` multiplies residual means from two independent batches and applies the one-half factor. A particular estimate can be negative although the true squared-error loss is non-negative. Clipping it would bias the estimator. Candidate and incumbent can share random numbers within factor A and within factor B to reduce the variance of their difference, while A and B remain independent of each other. Pixel correlations are retained by reducing each complete replicate to a scalar before estimating the replicate uncertainty.

There is also an objective-level explanation. The expected squared residual of the Monte Carlo image is

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\tfrac12(\widehat y-d)^2\right] =\tfrac12(y-d)^2 +\tfrac12\operatorname{Var}(\widehat y).
$$

<EquationNote term="d">fixed observed signal</EquationNote>

[Equation (10.21)](#equation-10-21) contains a penalty for simulation variance. If that variance depends on the parameters, minimising this expectation can favour parameters that are easier for the sampler. Increasing the photon budget changes the unwanted term. A solver should specify whether it targets a loss of the physical expectation or an expectation of a noisy loss.

Express selected density scales and source amplitude in absolute log coordinates. Each trial uploads that small vector, retains image A while replaying B for each derivative, and reduces the resulting loss gradient. For a candidate loss change, reuse each independent factor at both the incumbent and candidate.

Nonlinear likelihoods require their own analysis. For an ideal Poisson count $d$ with positive expected count $y$, the negative log-likelihood up to a data-only constant has derivative

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm P}=y-d\log y,\\
\mathcal L_{\mathrm P}' =\left(1-\frac d y\right)y'.
\end{gathered}
$$

Even with independent batches, replacing the reciprocal in [equation (10.22)](#equation-10-22) by $1/\widehat y$ generally gives bias: an unbiased estimate of $y$ does not have an unbiased reciprocal. An estimate equal to zero also makes that substitution undefined. Options include evaluating the mean accurately enough to control the resulting bias or deriving an estimator for the specific nonlinear objective. Flooring the noisy reciprocal changes the estimator and its bias. If the physical mean is floored in the objective itself, differentiate that explicitly modified objective. An energy-integrating detector needs the [observation model developed in Chapter 8](/chapters/spectral-transport-detector/#86-spatial-response-and-measurement-noise), rather than inheriting the Poisson formula from a photon counter.

Work in scaled parameter coordinates and choose optimisation steps in relation to the gradient uncertainty. Large steps cannot be justified merely because one noisy gradient has a large magnitude. Reusing histories over several nearby iterations can make the sampled objective easier to optimise, but fresh independent evaluations are needed to find out whether the expected objective improved. Increase the simulation budget when uncertainty prevents resolving a useful update, and keep acquisition noise separate from that numerical decision.

The deterministic strong-Wolfe search in [Chapter 6](/chapters/recovering-pose/#66-the-canonical-optimisation-loop) assumes repeated evaluations of the same objective and derivative. For stochastic transport, separate the histories used to propose a step, assess its loss change and check the returned point. `recover_expected_signal` uses a trust region to bound the proposed move. A linear model moves to its boundary; a quadratic model can use curvature to choose a smaller correction near a solution.

With `proposal="quadratic"`, minimise the local model $\widehat g^Ts+\tfrac12s^TBs$ inside the Euclidean ball $\lVert s\rVert\leq\Delta$. In one dimension with positive curvature $B$, an interior minimiser is $s=-\widehat g/B$, provided $|\widehat g/B|<\Delta$. Thus a smaller estimated slope produces a smaller step without first shrinking the trust radius. Keep the coordinates in the declared log-parameter chart. Estimate $B=\widehat J^TW\widehat J$ from the resident derivative images and reduce the Gram matrix on CUDA; only the small gradient and matrix need to reach the host solve.

Squaring a noisy Jacobian generally biases this curvature estimate. Use it to propose the step, retain independent residual and derivative factors in the gradient, and assess the candidate's loss change with separate histories. Inspect rank before damping: stabilising a singular matrix does not add information to the acquisition. Chen and colleagues analyse this separation of random models and step assessment under probabilistic accuracy conditions. The empirical error bands used here are a practical decision rule, without those convergence guarantees. <Citation keys={["chen2018stochastic"]} />

An interior step can be much smaller than the radius. If a candidate is rejected, reuse the incumbent's local model while contracting the radius, then draw fresh acceptance histories for the new candidate. If acceptance is inconclusive, hold the candidate fixed and increase the batch. Build a new model after an accepted move. Grow the radius only after a boundary-constrained step with sufficient agreement between predicted and estimated decrease.

Assess loss changes using a chosen multiple of the empirical standard error across independent scalar replicates. These bands are heuristic: repeated consultation does not give them simultaneous coverage. Under stochastic sampling, an all-zero gradient and error estimate can mean that no useful histories arrived. Increase the batch and return `sampling_unresolved` if the available budget cannot resolve the estimate. The deterministic absorption limit instead uses a separately declared numerical gradient allowance.

Reserve enough sampling budget to assess a candidate and check the returned point before spending on another model. Test provisional stationarity with fresh held-out gradients that never select a step. If that check fails, return an unresolved result. Keep this numerical stopping decision separate from the parameter-identifiability analysis in [Chapter 6](/chapters/recovering-pose/#65-single-view-ambiguity-and-identifiability).

### Sampling uncertainty and parameter error

If the gradient band crosses the tolerance, the sampling precision cannot resolve the stopping decision. Increasing the history count can narrow that band without changing the objective or relaxing the tolerance.

A small gradient can also coexist with appreciable density error when strong attenuation makes the signal insensitive to density. The measurement is weakly sensitive to density in this regime. Curvature can improve the proposed step, but a precise density estimate may still require a more informative acquisition.

Check predictions on withheld views or exposures, using fresh simulation histories. Fresh histories assess numerical error; withheld observations test whether the fitted model predicts another measurement. Inspect residuals and parameter sensitivities together. If source intensity can trade against attenuation, constrain one of them or add an observation that separates their effects.
