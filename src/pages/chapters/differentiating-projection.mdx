Move the estimated vertebra a fraction of a millimetre. Some rays acquire more bone, some lose it, and some barely change. A registration method needs the local effect of that movement on its mismatch with the measured image. Finite differences estimate the response from perturbed renders. Differentiation computes it without requiring a separate perturbed render for every parameter.

The exponential is the easy part. [Chapter 2](/chapters/transmission/#26-evaluating-transmission-numerically) already gave its derivative. Most of the work lies upstream: the pose changes sample locations, the interpolant changes beneath them, and clipping can move the integration interval. A plausible gradient image can omit one of those dependencies just as a plausible projection can contain a half-voxel error. The forward model is still the primary, fixed-energy measurement from the preceding chapters.

The pelvic example shows the projection and its six local count sensitivities for a synthetic anatomy under a fixed beam. Translation and rotation have different derivative units. The figures help us interpret the result, while the directional checks later in this chapter test the calculation.

<PoseSensitivityFigure id="pose-sensitivity" />

The function to build is `projection_vjp()`. Given the optical-depth cotangent from `transmission_vjp()`, it will accumulate derivatives for the active volume coefficients or six local pose coordinates. We will derive the contributions at one sample, assemble the reverse ray traversal, then compare its answer with independent perturbations. That gives [Chapter 6's optimiser](/chapters/recovering-pose/#66-the-canonical-optimisation-loop) the gradient of a complete rendered image.

## 5.1 Which projection derivative do we need?

Let $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ collect the active inputs: perhaps the six local pose coordinates, perhaps volume coefficients, perhaps both. Hold the other inputs fixed. The renderer produces a vector of expected primary counts, and a scalar objective compares that prediction with observed data $\mathbf{y}$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\\
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =\mathcal{D}\!\left(\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\mathbf{y}\right).
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol{\lambda}">
  Vector of expected primary detector counts.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol{\theta}">
  Active physical or material parameters of the renderer.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathbf{y}">Vector of observed detector data.</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathcal D">
  Scalar discrepancy between predicted and observed data.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.1)](#equation-5-1) separates the renderer's output from the comparison applied to it. Differentiating an expected count does not require sampling photons. A noise model matters when we choose $\mathcal D$ and interpret the observations.

If there are $P$ detector samples and $K$ active scalar parameters, the full Jacobian has $P K$ entries. Two products are usually more useful:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{J}_{\mathcal F} =\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}},\\
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{J}_{\mathcal F}\boldsymbol{\eta},\\
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{J}_{\mathcal F}^{\mathsf T}\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\mathbf{J}_{\mathcal F}">
  Jacobian of expected counts with respect to the active parameters.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol{\eta}">
  Direction in the active parameter coordinates.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}">
  Detector-space cotangent supplied to the reverse calculation.
</EquationNote>

In [equation (5.2)](#equation-5-2), a Jacobian-vector product, or JVP, predicts the image change along the parameter direction $\boldsymbol{\eta}$. A vector-Jacobian product, or VJP, carries the detector weights $\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}$ back to the inputs. In this chapter's reverse rules, an overbar denotes a cotangent: the sensitivity of a downstream scalar to that variable.

For the objective above, choose $\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathcal D$. Then $\mathbf g$ is the parameter gradient. Reverse mode is attractive when one scalar depends on many inputs. With only six pose parameters, a small set of directional derivatives can also be practical. The mathematical requirement is the same product, regardless of the differentiation strategy.

Keep the VJP interface explicit about its derivative coordinates. `adj_L` has the same shape and precision as the optical-depth output. For pose, `out_pose` holds six binary64 cotangents of a local right increment. For the field, `out_mu` holds binary32 coefficient cotangents. A separate `out_pose_matrix` option returns twelve partials with respect to stored transform entries for a tape chain rule. Use one pose representation per call. Grid and acquisition calibration remain fixed.

A derivative with respect to translation has count per millimetre units, while one with respect to rotation has count per radian units. To compare or optimise scaled coordinates, write a local increment as

$$
\begin{gathered}
\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}=\mathbf{D}\,\delta\mathbf{z},\\
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf z}=\mathbf{J}_{\mathcal F}\mathbf{D},\\
\nabla_{\mathbf z}\Phi=\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf T}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Phi.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\mathbf D">
  Diagonal matrix converting dimensionless increments to physical parameter
  increments.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathbf z">
  Dimensionless local parameter coordinates.
</EquationNote>

Here $\mathbf z$ is dimensionless and the diagonal matrix $\mathbf D$ supplies declared physical step scales. [Equation (5.3)](#equation-5-3) changes the coordinates of the derivative, but it does not add information to the image. Pose increments still compose with the current transform on the side chosen in Chapter 3.

## 5.2 Attenuation and transmission derivatives

For one detector sample, the expected primary count is $\lambda_p=n_{0,p}\exp(-\widehat L_p)$. Let both optical depth and open-beam expectation be active. Their differential is

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathrm d\lambda_p =T_p\,\mathrm dn_{0,p} -\lambda_p\,\mathrm d\widehat L_p,\\
\mathrm dT_p=-T_p\,\mathrm d\widehat L_p.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="T_p">
  Probability of primary survival along detector ray $p$.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="n_{0,p}">
  Expected detector photons in the absence of the object.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\lambda_p">
  Expected primary detector count after attenuation.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.4)](#equation-5-4) supplies two distinct paths for an image change. Increasing illumination raises expected counts, while increasing attenuation lowers them. Holding the open beam fixed removes the first term. If it is itself calibrated by active acquisition parameters, that dependency remains part of the derivative.

For a fixed ray and sample pattern, [Chapter 4's linear optical-depth operator](/chapters/volumes-line-integrals/#equation-4-17) gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widehat L_p=\sum_v W_{pv}a_v,\\
\frac{\partial\widehat L_p}{\partial a_v}=W_{pv},\\
\frac{\partial\lambda_p}{\partial a_v}=-\lambda_p W_{pv}.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="W_{pv}">
  Fixed-geometry path and interpolation weight, in mm.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="a_v">
  Stored attenuation coefficient, in $\mathrm{mm}^{-1}$.
</EquationNote>

The coefficient $W_{pv}$ in [equation (5.5)](#equation-5-5) has units of millimetres and includes both interpolation weights and physical path weights. It is zero when the ray's sampled basis support never reaches coefficient $v$. Increasing a nonnegative coefficient cannot increase the expected primary count for this field model.

The corresponding reverse rules are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\overline L_p=-\lambda_p\overline\lambda_p,\\
\overline n_{0,p}=T_p\overline\lambda_p,\\
\overline a_v=\sum_p W_{pv}\overline L_p.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\overline L_p">
  Cotangent of the dimensionless optical depth.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\overline n_{0,p}">
  Cotangent of the expected open-beam count.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\overline a_v">
  Cotangent accumulated for volume coefficient $v$.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.6)](#equation-5-6) turns an image-space cotangent into an attenuation-field gradient. If one open-beam scalar is shared across pixels, its cotangent is the sum of the per-pixel contributions. A per-pixel illumination array has a different derivative shape, even if all its values happen to be equal.

Strong attenuation makes $\lambda_p$ small. For a fixed, bounded count-space seed, its contribution to $\overline L_p$ then becomes small too. The objective can alter that seed substantially. With known positive $n_{0,p}$, the Poisson negative log-likelihood, after dropping terms independent of $L_p$, has

$$
\begin{gathered}
\Phi_p(L_p)=n_{0,p}e^{-L_p}+y_pL_p,\\
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi_p}{\mathrm dL_p}=y_p-\lambda_p.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="y_p">
  Observed photon count at detector sample $p$.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\Phi_p">
  Per-pixel Poisson objective with terms independent of optical depth omitted.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.7)](#equation-5-7) can be evaluated without forming $1-y_p/\lambda_p$, which becomes numerically troublesome when the predicted count is tiny. Its shortened objective assumes fixed illumination. Fitting $n_{0,p}$ requires restoring the terms that depend on it. This is the same statistical distinction used in Chapter 2.

Derivatives must also retain the forward numerical policy. Computing a weighted derivative from an already-underflowed stored transmission can lose a representable result when the cotangent is large. Recompute the required exponential product with the declared precision and range, as for expected counts. The derivative of the intended smooth formula is the target, with floating-point error measured against it.

Pass the original depths and illumination to the transmission VJP, then use its depth destination as `adj_L`. In a pose-only fit the volume is fixed, so the projection pass needs only a six-coordinate reduction. Activating volume coefficients adds their gradient scatter to the same ray traversal.

The count-space composition stores an intermediate mean and derivative seed. A positive observation paired with a mean rounded to zero, or an unrepresentable seed, must therefore raise an error. For a wider numerical range, compose the Poisson loss directly through optical depth using equation (5.7). [Chapter 6](/chapters/recovering-pose/#66-the-canonical-optimisation-loop) uses this connection in its binary64 evaluator, avoiding the stored count-space seed.

## 5.3 Geometry through the sampled field

A pose update moves the ray in object coordinates while the volume coefficients stay attached to the anatomy. For a fixed world point $\mathbf{x}^W$, let $\mathbf{x}^O=\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{x}^W-\mathbf{t})$. Inverting the [left and right updates from Chapter 3](/chapters/frames-geometry-motion/#36-pose-parameters-and-local-updates) gives the first-order motions

$$
\begin{gathered}
\delta\mathbf{x}^O_{\mathrm{left}} =-\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\rho}_W {}-\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf T}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_W\times\mathbf{x}^W),\\
\delta\mathbf{x}^O_{\mathrm{right}} =-\boldsymbol{\rho}_O-\boldsymbol{\phi}_O\times\mathbf{x}^O.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol{\rho}_W">
  Translational coordinate of a left pose increment, expressed in world mm.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol{\phi}_W">
  Rotation-vector coordinate of a left pose increment, in world axes and
  radians.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol{\rho}_O">
  Translational coordinate of a right pose increment, in object mm.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\boldsymbol{\phi}_O">
  Rotation-vector coordinate of a right pose increment, in object axes and
  radians.
</EquationNote>

The minus signs in [equation (5.8)](#equation-5-8) arise because we query the inverse pose. Translating the object to the right makes a fixed world ray visit object points farther to the left. These expressions differ from the active point motions in Chapter 3 because the point being held fixed is different.

Within an open trilinear interpolation cell, coefficient differences give the gradient with respect to grid coordinates. For example,

$$
\frac{\partial\mu_A}{\partial u_x} =\sum_{b,c\in\{0,1\}} \left[ A[m_z+c,m_y+b,m_x+1] {}-A[m_z+c,m_y+b,m_x] \right] {}\times w_b(\alpha_y)w_c(\alpha_z).
$$

<EquationNote term="u_x">
  Continuous grid coordinate along the first volume axis.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\alpha_y">
  Fractional position along the second interpolation axis.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\alpha_z">
  Fractional position along the third interpolation axis.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.9)](#equation-5-9) differentiates the interpolation weights while the selected cell stays fixed. The other components follow by exchanging directions. The zero-extended reference uses zero outside coefficients. The implemented [half-cell sampler](/chapters/volumes-line-integrals/#listing-4-2) instead suppresses the slope along a clamped edge axis, and the interior formula is unchanged. Multiplying by $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{S}^{-1}$ converts this grid gradient into $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^O}\mu_A$ with units $\mathrm{mm}^{-2}$.

Now let one scalar parameter be $\theta$. For a fixed sample count in a region where the clipping and interpolation branches are differentiable, the discrete optical-depth derivative is

$$
\frac{\partial\widehat L_p}{\partial\theta} =\sum_r \left[ \frac{\partial w_{pr}}{\partial\theta}\mu_A(\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O) {}+w_{pr}\nabla\mu_A(\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O)^{\mathsf T} \frac{\partial\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O}{\partial\theta} \right].
$$

<EquationNote term="\theta">One active scalar parameter.</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="w_{pr}">
  Physical quadrature weight of sample $r$ on ray $p$, in mm.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O">
  Physical sample position in object coordinates, in mm.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.10)](#equation-5-10) displays both ways geometry changes a quadrature: it changes where we sample and how much physical distance each sample represents. This expression holds coefficients fixed. Active coefficient changes add the linear contribution from equation (5.5).

For samples interpolated between object-space endpoints, their derivative is

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O}{\partial\theta} =(1-t_{pr})\frac{\partial\mathbf{s}^O}{\partial\theta} {}+t_{pr}\frac{\partial\mathbf{q}_p^O}{\partial\theta} {}+(\mathbf{q}_p^O-\mathbf{s}^O) \frac{\partial t_{pr}}{\partial\theta}.
$$

<EquationNote term="t_{pr}">
  Dimensionless interpolation parameter locating sample $r$ between the ray
  endpoints.
</EquationNote>

The last term in [equation (5.11)](#equation-5-11) vanishes for a sample parameter fixed on the full source-to-detector segment. It generally remains when sample positions are redistributed over a pose-dependent clipped interval. Detaching the clipping result from automatic differentiation discards that motion.

Use the entry and exit derivatives returned by `finite_interval()` in [Listing 4.3](/chapters/volumes-line-integrals/#listing-4-3). A bound fixed at the source or detector has zero derivative with respect to object motion. Otherwise, the active box face contributes to sample motion. Include this contribution even for a constant field, because the physical length of the interval can still change.

For equal midpoint quadrature with fixed $M_p$, write $c_r=(r+1/2)/M_p$. Differentiating the actual node and weight definitions gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
\partial_\theta t_{pr} =(1-c_r)\partial_\theta t_p^- {}+c_r\partial_\theta t_p^+,\\
\partial_\theta w_{pr} =\frac{t_p^+-t_p^-}{M_p}\partial_\theta d_p {}+\frac{d_p}{M_p}(\partial_\theta t_p^+-\partial_\theta t_p^-).
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="c_r">
  Fixed midpoint fraction within the clipped interval.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="t_p^-">Ray parameter at the clipped entry.</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="t_p^+">Ray parameter at the clipped exit.</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.12)](#equation-5-12) makes the clipping dependency explicit ([Figure](#figure-5-1)). Rigid object motion preserves the source-to-detector distance, so $\partial_\theta d_p=0$ for those parameters. Moving source or detector parameters can change it. The active box face determines the derivative of each clipped endpoint away from ties.

## 5.4 Discretise and differentiate

We have differentiated a finite weighted sum. The derivative of a continuous line integral provides another useful comparison, but it has its own hypotheses. Suppose $f(t,\theta)$ is differentiable on the changing interval, with enough regularity to differentiate under the integral. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\theta} \int_{a(\theta)}^{b(\theta)}f(t,\theta)\,\mathrm dt ={}\int_a^b\partial_\theta f(t,\theta)\,\mathrm dt {}+f(b,\theta)b'(\theta) {}-f(a,\theta)a'(\theta).
$$

<EquationNote term="f">
  Parameter-dependent integrand on the moving interval.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="a">
  Lower integration bound as a function of the active parameter.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="b">
  Upper integration bound as a function of the active parameter.
</EquationNote>

For our ray, $f=d_p\mu_A(\mathbf{x}_p^O(t))$. The endpoint terms in [equation (5.13)](#equation-5-13) account for changing integration bounds. At an exterior support face of the continuous zero-extended hat field, the field value is zero, so that face's continuous endpoint term vanishes. Differentiating a finite midpoint sum still requires its moving weights and sample positions: those terms combine to approximate the continuous derivative.

The implemented clamped field can have a nonzero trace at its outer half-cell face. Its continuous endpoint term then remains, as does the corresponding contribution from the changing finite quadrature. This is one reason to keep the zero-extended reference and the executable field distinct: importing the reference's vanishing endpoint term would remove a real sensitivity from the code's declared model.

A sharp boundary shows why the distinction matters. Consider a one-dimensional material of coefficient $\mu_0>0$ occupying $[a,b]$ inside a fixed ray segment, with $b$ fixed and $a$ moving. Its integral is

$$
\begin{gathered}
L(a)=\mu_0(b-a),\\
\frac{\mathrm dL}{\mathrm da}=-\mu_0.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\mu_0">
  Uniform material attenuation in the interval, in $\mathrm{mm}^{-1}$.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="a">Moving physical entry position, in mm.</EquationNote>

The boundary derivative in [equation (5.14)](#equation-5-14) is nonzero even though the coefficient is constant everywhere inside the material. Sampling its hard indicator at a fixed finite set of points produces a staircase as $a$ moves: automatic differentiation of each selected branch returns zero between sample crossings. That is a derivative of the sampled staircase, and it does not approximate the moving-boundary derivative pointwise by merely increasing the number of samples.

Exact cell traversal represents boundary motion through changing segment lengths. A continuous interpolant represents a softened boundary through spatial gradients over a finite width. Each can support a useful derivative, but they differentiate different finite-resolution fields. The boundary convention changes the gradient seen by the inverse problem.

Gopalakrishnan and Golland's DiffDRR formulation vectorises Siddon's intersections and length-weighted sums in PyTorch, so automatic differentiation follows those tensor operations. <Citation keys={["gopalakrishnan2023diffdrr"]} /> Our sampled trilinear projector exposes a different set of intermediates: basis weights, spatial gradients and quadrature bounds. Writing its VJP explicitly lets us recompute those quantities inside a ray loop instead of retaining every sample. The choice concerns both the represented field and the storage needed to differentiate it.

The sample-count rule adds another discrete decision. If $M_p=\lceil\ell_p/h\rceil$, changing pose can change both the number and positions of samples. At the transition, two different midpoint rules need not produce the same value. Within a fixed-count branch, equation (5.10) differentiates the selected quadrature. It does not describe a derivative across that transition.

One option is to keep sample counts fixed during each local optimisation stage and refine the discretisation between stages. Another is to use fixed sample parameters on the complete finite ray, accepting extra samples in zero-valued regions. Either choice has computational consequences. What matters for gradient verification is that the perturbed forward evaluations and the derivative use the same declared discretisation.

## 5.5 Reverse-mode differentiation in Warp

First obtain the cotangent of each optical depth from the detector objective. Then revisit the contributing samples. Each sample sends a contribution to the volume coefficients it read and, when geometry is active, a contribution through its position and weight to the pose parameters.

For one trilinear sample, with basis value $B_v(\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O)$ for coefficient $v$, the local reverse contributions are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\overline a_v\mathrel{+}=\overline L_p w_{pr}B_v(\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O),\\
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{pr}^O =\overline L_p w_{pr}\nabla\mu_A(\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O),\\
\overline w_{pr}=\overline L_p\mu_A(\mathbf{x}_{pr}^O).
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="B_v">
  Dimensionless interpolation basis associated with coefficient $v$.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{pr}^O">
  Cotangent of the physical sample position.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\overline w_{pr}">
  Cotangent of the sample integration weight.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.15)](#equation-5-15) is an execution guide for a VJP. Many rays can contribute to one volume coefficient, so those writes require a correct accumulation strategy. Pose-only differentiation avoids the volume-gradient scatter, but still needs to reduce per-ray contributions into a small shared parameter vector.

With $P=1024^2$ rays and $M=512$ samples per ray, storing only three binary32 position coordinates per sample uses 6 GiB. Coefficient indices, interpolation weights and their adjoints add to that total. Reconstructing positions from the endpoints and sample index can replace that retained tensor with arithmetic in the backward pass.

A recomputed backward must reproduce the forward's clipping, interpolation, sampling and arithmetic policies. If its branch selection differs because inputs were mutated or precision changed, it can scatter cotangents into different coefficients. Retain the original inputs and any branch decisions needed for exact replay, or prove that recomputation obtains them under the recorded configuration.

A generated backward pass still needs the forward intermediates consumed by its derivative. Warp's [differentiation guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/warp/v1.17.0/docs/user_guide/differentiability.rst) describes both custom gradient functions and replay restrictions for dynamic loops. We use an explicit reverse traversal so that each sample's position, cell and weights are available exactly where they are needed. The local derivatives are equation (5.15); the remaining task is to accumulate them in the right coordinates.

Reconstruct the forward midpoint sequence in the VJP kernel. Accumulate contributions separately for the ray origin, direction and two interval bounds. `lower_seed` and `upper_seed` combine sample motion with the change in physical interval length. Finally, apply the transpose of `object_to_grid` to return to physical object coordinates, then contract with the right-pose derivatives.

The flags passed to `get_vjp()` specialise the kernel when it is prepared. A field-only VJP needs basis weights but no field-value reads or pose reduction. A pose-only VJP needs values and spatial slopes but no scatter into a volume gradient. `wp.static` removes the unrequested work from each specialisation, so the flags do not branch separately for every ray at runtime.

Reduce pose contributions in two stages: one binary64 partial vector per block, then a reduction to six or twelve entries. Padded lanes contribute zero and still participate. Volume coefficients need a different strategy because many rays update the same locations, so that path uses binary32 scatter atomics. Their order can vary, and overflow must be checked after accumulation. A pose-only call avoids this scatter.

To place the operator in a larger differentiable computation, record its forward calls with `wp.Tape` and create participating arrays with `requires_grad=True`. `Tape.backward(grads=...)` supplies the output cotangent. Its shape, dtype and device must match the output. Keep forward inputs alive and unchanged until reverse execution finishes, and allocate reusable output and gradient buffers before the optimisation loop.

## 5.6 Independent directional checks

Choose a base pose and a direction in dimensionless scaled coordinates $\mathbf z$. Build perturbed poses using the same left or right composition rule as the analytic derivative. For a vector output, a central difference estimates the JVP:

$$
\mathbf{v}_h =\frac{\mathcal F(\mathbf z+h\boldsymbol{\eta}) -\mathcal F(\mathbf z-h\boldsymbol{\eta})}{2h}.
$$

<EquationNote term="\mathbf v_h">
  Central-difference estimate of the image JVP at step $h$.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="h">
  Dimensionless perturbation step in scaled parameter coordinates.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.16)](#equation-5-16) uses a dimensionless step $h$, and the physical perturbations come from the scaling matrix in equation (5.3). For pose parameters, the notation means composing the scaled increment with the base pose, not adding entries to a rotation matrix.

For the implemented VJP, hold a detector seed fixed and take its dot product with each perturbed forward output before forming the central difference. Compare that scalar difference with the dot product of the VJP cotangent, expressed in the same scaled parameter coordinates, and the chosen direction. Both calculations then predict the change in the same seeded scalar, so this check needs neither a stored image Jacobian nor a separately implemented JVP. The seed must correspond to the output being checked: optical depth for the projector alone, or expected counts for the composed rendering chain.

Check a range of steps. In a smooth region, central-difference truncation error decreases quadratically as $h$ shrinks, until subtraction and floating-point errors dominate. One favourable step size can conceal a wrong derivative. A useful discrepancy measure is

$$
\epsilon_h =\frac{\lVert\mathbf v_h-\mathbf v\rVert_2} {\max(s_0,\lVert\mathbf v_h\rVert_2,\lVert\mathbf v\rVert_2)}.
$$

<EquationNote term="\epsilon_h">
  Relative JVP discrepancy with a declared absolute scale.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="s_0">
  Positive reference scale in the same units as the image derivative.
</EquationNote>

The scale $s_0$ in [equation (5.17)](#equation-5-17) has the same units as the image derivative. It prevents an almost-zero reference from turning a harmless absolute error into a large relative ratio. Report the absolute error too. The normalisation must not conceal a materially wrong signal change.

A scalar Taylor test avoids dividing two nearly equal output values by a very small step. For a differentiable scalar objective and direction, form

$$
R(h)=\left| \Phi(\mathbf z+h\boldsymbol{\eta})-\Phi(\mathbf z) {}-h\nabla\Phi(\mathbf z)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\eta} \right|.
$$

<EquationNote term="R(h)">
  Absolute remainder after subtracting the predicted first-order objective
  change.
</EquationNote>

With sufficient local smoothness, [equation (5.18)](#equation-5-18) has a quadratic remainder. Leaving the linear term out gives a first-order change when the directional derivative is nonzero. Seeing those two slopes separates a meaningful derivative correction from an objective that is simply insensitive to the chosen direction.

Analytic fields provide references that share no interpolation code with the implementation. For $\mu(\mathbf{x}^O)=\mu_0+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}^O$ along a finite segment entirely inside an affine interpolation region,

$$
\begin{gathered}
L_p=d_p\left[\mu_0+ \mathbf b^{\mathsf T}\frac{\mathbf s^O+\mathbf q_p^O}{2}\right],\\
\nabla_{\mathbf t}L_p=-d_p\mathbf R\mathbf b.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="\mathbf b">
  Constant spatial attenuation gradient of the analytic affine field, in
  $\mathrm{mm}^{-2}$.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathbf t">
  Object translation expressed in world coordinates, in mm.
</EquationNote>

The translation derivative in [equation (5.19)](#equation-5-19) holds the world ray and object rotation fixed and differentiates with respect to world translation. The vector $\mathbf b$ has units $\mathrm{mm}^{-2}$. Choose $\mu_0$ so attenuation remains nonnegative on the tested neighbourhood. This case checks inverse-pose signs, the physical spacing conversion and distance weights without a moving support boundary.

Test the volume derivative separately using coefficient directions and the fixed-geometry linear operator. Then test pure translations, pure rotations and mixed directions. Mixed directions matter: separate component checks can miss an error in the way shared intermediate contributions are accumulated.

Compare two finite-difference sequences: the actual stored forward outputs and an independent higher-precision evaluation of the same discrete formula. Their small-step error floors can differ because the first includes output rounding. This distinction helps separate a wrong derivative from a perturbation too small for the stored image.

Include a small transverse slope beside a large field background. This tests whether the sampler's corner differences preserve a derivative that could disappear if two large interpolated values were subtracted instead. An ordinary smooth field may not expose that cancellation.

## 5.7 Boundaries, conditioning and failure cases

Between its exterior support faces, a trilinear field is continuous but not generally differentiable at its knot planes. The implemented field can additionally jump at a nonzero half-cell boundary. In one dimension, let adjacent slopes on either side of a sample centre be $g_-$ and $g_+$. At the knot,

$$
\begin{gathered}
\lim_{h\downarrow0}\frac{f(0+h)-f(0)}{h}=g_+,\\
\lim_{h\downarrow0}\frac{f(0)-f(0-h)}{h}=g_-.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="g_-">
  Slope approaching the interpolation knot from below.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="g_+">
  Slope leaving the knot in the positive direction.
</EquationNote>

If the two values in [equation (5.20)](#equation-5-20) differ, no ordinary derivative exists there. A symmetric finite difference tends to their average, while an executed interpolation branch may report either one-sided slope. Agreement with that average is therefore not the acceptance criterion for a branch derivative. Move the base point into a regular cell for a smooth test, and test the knot with its stated one-sided behaviour.

A physical tangency can also create a large derivative. For a homogeneous sphere of radius $R$ and a line whose perpendicular distance from the centre is $b<R$, assume the finite source-detector segment contains the whole chord. Then

$$
\begin{gathered}
L(b)=2\mu\sqrt{R^2-b^2},\\
\frac{\mathrm dL}{\mathrm db} =-\frac{2\mu b}{\sqrt{R^2-b^2}}.
\end{gathered}
$$

<EquationNote term="R">Radius of the homogeneous sphere, in mm.</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="b">
  Perpendicular distance of the ray line from the sphere centre, in mm.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.21)](#equation-5-21) diverges as the line approaches tangency from inside. This is a property of the sharp sphere model. A sampled smooth boundary can regularise it at the cost of changing that model. A large gradient near a boundary and a missing gradient caused by a detached clipping operation require different repairs.

A uniform region may barely change when translated along directions that preserve the traversed material. Symmetry can make several poses indistinguishable. Strong attenuation can suppress count-space sensitivity. The derivative can be correct and small in all these cases. Adding a numerical floor to force a gradient invents a response that the forward model does not have.

Use spatially asymmetric fields, unequal spacings and off-centre rotation pivots to expose coordinate errors. Also include deliberately insensitive cases, where the expected derivative is zero. Tests consisting only of large nonzero gradients will miss accidental offsets and spurious boundary responses.

During debugging, inspect the first layer at which agreement is lost: point motion, interpolated field value, optical depth, detector expectation or scalar objective. A forward finite difference of the final loss combines all of them. Layered analytic cases make the sign or scale error much easier to locate without lowering the final acceptance standard.

## 5.8 Gradient validation as an executable contract

Before testing a derivative, fix the forward map and its parameter coordinates. For this renderer that means the right-pose update, physical units, field extension and quadrature used throughout the chapter. Check the same map in every perturbation.

For any consistent JVP and VJP under Euclidean inner products, parameter direction $\boldsymbol\eta$ and detector seed $\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}$ satisfy

$$
\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf J_{\mathcal F}\boldsymbol\eta) =\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf J_{\mathcal F}^{\mathsf T}\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}).
$$

<EquationNote term="\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}">
  Detector-space seed used in the adjoint consistency test.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.22)](#equation-5-22) checks adjoint consistency without storing the Jacobian. Use several independent, asymmetric directions and seeds. This check catches missing accumulation terms and mismatched interpolation weights, but two mutually consistent implementations of the wrong operator can still pass. The analytic forward and directional checks remain necessary.

If the desired inner products include quadrature or voxel-volume weights, the corresponding adjoint changes. With positive definite weighting matrices $\mathbf M_\theta$ and $\mathbf M_y$,

$$
\mathbf J_{\mathcal F}^{*} =\mathbf M_\theta^{-1}\mathbf J_{\mathcal F}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf M_y.
$$

<EquationNote term="\mathbf M_\theta^{-1}">
  Inverse of the positive definite matrix defining the parameter-space inner
  product.
</EquationNote>

<EquationNote term="\mathbf M_y">
  Positive definite matrix defining the detector-space inner product.
</EquationNote>

[Equation (5.23)](#equation-5-23) distinguishes that weighted adjoint from the ordinary array transpose computed by a Euclidean VJP. Introduce such weights through the objective and parameter convention deliberately. Multiplying by a voxel volume in one direction only breaks the identity being tested.

Keep the chosen conventions and complete perturbation sweep with the result.

Once the formulas agree, repeat the composed reverse pass with reused buffers and a new seed. A missing reset or a lost shared-input contribution can survive an isolated derivative test.

You now have the forward chain from pose to counts and the reverse chain from an image cotangent back to pose or material coefficients. [Chapter 6](/chapters/recovering-pose/) supplies the data term and update rule, using these operators repeatedly to recover an unknown pose.
